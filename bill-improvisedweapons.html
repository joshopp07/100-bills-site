The Drones and Explosives Act

The Drones and Explosives Act makes it a federal felony to fly a drone armed with a weapon, and selling kits built to arm a drone is a crime too, whatever the listing calls them. Flying a drone with no weapon on it stays as legal as it is today. Certified security staff at stadiums and power plants can stop a hostile drone over their grounds, and the counter-drone powers police and prisons already hold no longer expire. Buying chemicals used in homemade bombs above set strengths means showing photo ID, in a store or online, and chemical-plant security rules that lapsed in 2023 come back for good.

  • Flying a drone armed with a gun, a bomb, or another dangerous weapon is a federal felony punishable by up to five years in prison, rising to life if the attack kills someone.
  • Flying a drone with no weapon on it stays as legal as it is now, and the bill creates no new list of drone owners.
  • Buying hydrogen peroxide, nitric acid, nitromethane, chlorates, or perchlorates above set strengths and amounts means showing photo ID, in a store or online, and a buyer of ammonium nitrate has to register and show ID.
  • Selling a drop mechanism or weapon mount for a drone is a crime whatever the listing calls it, online marketplaces have to pull such listings within 48 hours of notice, and certified crop-spraying and industrial equipment can still be sold.
  • Certified security staff at stadiums, power plants, and other critical sites can track a drone that threatens their site and, when the threat is imminent, stop it over their grounds, and the counter-drone powers of police and prison officers no longer expire in 2031.
