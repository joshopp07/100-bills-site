The Make Private Jets Pay Act

Today, a private jet flown by its owner pays no ticket tax, while every passenger on an airliner flying the same route pays one. The Make Private Jets Pay Act makes airlines, cargo carriers, and private jets pay for the air traffic control and runway space they use, on one schedule. The bill ends the taxes on domestic airline tickets and replaces them with charges on each flight. Short private-jet hops owe a set minimum charge, businesses can no longer write off private planes and executive helicopters, and takeoff and landing rights at Reagan National, LaGuardia, and JFK are auctioned as a pilot, with the results reported to Congress as evidence for expanding or ending it.

  • The taxes on domestic airline tickets, including the 7.5 percent tax on the fare and the per-segment tax, end, replaced by per-flight charges that airline, cargo, and private-jet flights pay on one schedule.
  • Every private-jet flight owes at least a minimum charge equal to what it costs air traffic control to handle one takeoff and one landing, so a short private-jet trip pays its share.
  • Businesses can no longer deduct the cost of buying, maintaining, or flying a private plane or a helicopter that carries executives or employees for convenience, while flights that do the work itself, such as firefighting, farming, emergency medical transport, power-line repair, and carrying cargo, keep the deduction.
  • Takeoff and landing rights at Reagan National, LaGuardia, and JFK, now kept by the airlines that already hold them, are auctioned, a fifth of them each year during the changeover, and the results are reported to Congress as evidence for expanding the auctions to other airports or ending these auctions.
  • Scheduled passenger flights to and from remote airports with no paved road to another airport do not owe the new charges.
