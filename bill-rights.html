The Enforceable Rights Act

If a government official violates your rights, you should be able to go to court, hear a judge say you were wronged, and be paid for the harm. The Enforceable Rights Act gives that right to every person in the United States, whatever their citizenship or immigration status. You can sue the agency or department that employs the official, so the cost falls on the institution responsible, and the qualified-immunity defense that blocks many lawsuits against officials today ends for every defendant. Small violations by federal officers are paid through a simple claim form with no lawyer needed, and if the agency misses its deadline to answer, the claim is paid.

  • You can peacefully record any officer on duty in public, and an officer cannot stop or arrest you for that alone, make you delete the video, or take your phone over it without a warrant.
  • An officer stopping, searching, or arresting you in public has to show the agency’s name and a personal name or number, even when wearing a mask, and tell you both if you ask.
  • Before asking to search you or your home without a warrant, an officer has to tell you that you may say no, and saying no cannot be a reason to stop, search, or arrest you.
  • Officers can use force only when it is necessary and in proportion to the threat, cannot use a chokehold unless deadly force is allowed, and have to call for medical help for anyone hurt by force.
  • Anyone in government custody, including in a privately run jail, prison, or immigration detention center, gets medical, dental, and mental health care for serious needs at no cost. A facility that cannot provide that care has to send the person to one that can, or ask a court to release the person on conditions.
