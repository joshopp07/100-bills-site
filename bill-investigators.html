The Independent Investigators Act

When a federal officer kills or seriously injures someone, the state where it happened should not have to wait on the federal government to see the evidence. The Independent Investigators Act puts federal agencies on a clock to give state investigators access to witnesses, the scene, and the evidence. Inspectors general, the watchdogs inside federal agencies, cannot be pushed out on a vague claim of lost confidence. And when someone applying to be a federal prosecutor or agent is asked about their politics, the question goes on the record and the answer does not.

  • When a federal officer kills or seriously injures someone, or a person dies in federal custody, state investigators get access to witnesses the same day, the scene within 72 hours, and physical evidence, including body-camera video, within 7 days, and only a federal judge can give an agency more time. The federal government cannot tamper with any of it at any point: it cannot alter or destroy the scene or the evidence, delete a witness’s recording, or pressure or reward a witness to hold back or change their account. In a civil lawsuit over the incident, evidence lost in federal hands is presumed to have been unfavorable to the federal government.
  • The autopsy, forensic results, and recordings go to state investigators whether or not any federal charge is brought.
  • Federal agents cannot move a witness out of the state without first telling state investigators and giving them access to that witness.
  • A firing of an inspector general explained only by a loss of confidence, or by a reason that would fit any inspector general, does not take effect, and the reasons given for any firing are made public.
  • If you apply for a job as a federal prosecutor or law-enforcement agent, any question about your party, your vote, or your political views is written down with the name of the person who asked, and your answer is not.
