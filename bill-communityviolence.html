The Community Violence Reduction Act

The Community Violence Reduction Act pays, every year and without a grant cycle running out, for the outreach workers and hospital counselors who work to stop the next shooting, and every funded program’s results are published each year. It helps people at risk of suicide keep a gun out of reach in a crisis. A person can get a free gun lock with no questions asked, and can put their own name on a do-not-sell list and take it off later. Medicare and Medicaid can pay for a caseworker for a gunshot survivor. Federal research into why Americans are shot is funded every year, and crime-gun trace data is published.

  • Street outreach, hospital-based, and group violence intervention programs draw on permanent federal funding that does not wait on a yearly vote in Congress, and a program whose model is found to show no effect gets no increase.
  • Medicare and Medicaid can pay for a trained caseworker to help a person treated for a gunshot or other violent injury avoid being hurt again, and a Medicaid patient pays nothing for that care.
  • A person can get a free gun lock, with no questions asked, at a veterans’ health, military, or Indian Health Service clinic, a community health center, or a participating doctor’s office, and a gun bought from a dealer comes with a lock or other secure storage device.
  • A person can put their own name on a free do-not-sell list before a crisis, effective at once when made online and removable on 21 days’ notice. Nobody else can add them, and signing up or declining cannot be used against them.
  • Federal research into why Americans are shot receives $1.2 billion a year, the national system that tracks violent deaths runs in every state, and crime-gun trace data is published each year without naming any gun owner.
