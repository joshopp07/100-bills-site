The Fair Representation Act

Since 1967, federal law has required every House district to elect one member. A narrow win takes the only seat, and the voters on the other side elect no one. The Fair Representation Act changes that. States with six or more House members elect them from districts of three, four, or five seats, states with two to five elect theirs statewide, and in both the seats are shared in proportion to the votes. Voters in the minority where they live can still elect someone who speaks for them. The changes begin after the 2030 census.

  • In a district that elects more than one member, any political party can nominate up to as many candidates as there are seats, and candidates without a party can run on their own, getting on the ballot with no more than 25 signatures and no filing fee.
  • Each voter can vote for as many candidates as there are seats, or fewer, from one party or from several. A voter can also write in the name of someone who is not on the ballot, and the vote counts even if that person filed nothing before the election. A write-in candidate who wins a seat must accept it in writing within seven days of being notified, or the seat goes to the party or independent candidate next in line in the count. Candidates printed on the ballot accepted any seat they win when they qualified for the ballot.
  • The winners are decided in three steps. Step 1: each ballot counts as one vote, split among the parties in proportion to the candidates the voter chose, and each independent candidate counts as a party of one. In a district with five seats, a voter who chooses only Republicans, whether one or five of them, casts one full vote for the Republicans. A voter who chooses one Republican, two Democrats, and two independents casts one-fifth of a vote for the Republicans, two-fifths for the Democrats, and one-fifth for each independent.
  • Step 2: the shares from every ballot are added up, and the seats are divided among the parties and independent candidates in proportion to their totals, with no minimum share needed to win a seat.
  • Step 3: each party’s seats go to its candidates with the most votes, and every candidate a voter chose gets one full vote. The split in step 1 counts only toward how many seats each party wins, so a voter who chose four Republicans and one Democrat gives one full vote to each of those five candidates.
  • Your state cannot redraw its congressional map between one census and the next unless a court or the law requires it.
