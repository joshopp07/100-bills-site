The Day in Court Act

Federal immigration law lets a person facing deportation have a lawyer only "at no expense to the Government." The Day in Court Act pays for that lawyer when a child, a person who cannot understand the proceedings, or a person whose legal status the government ended cannot afford one. Your case in immigration court is decided by an independent judge, a person and not a computer. Proof that a letter was mailed is no longer enough to order you deported for missing a hearing. If you are held in detention, a judge keeps checking whether holding you is still justified, and you are released if the government cannot show that it is.

  • If you are in the United States, the government cannot deport you by claiming that your lawful beliefs, statements, or associations hurt American foreign policy.
  • You cannot be sent to a country that is not yours unless that country agrees in writing to take you and says in writing whether it would jail you there, and if a court finds you were deported unlawfully, the government has to take every step it can to bring you back.
  • Telling any officer, in any words or any language, that you fear being sent back puts your case before a judge instead of on the fast track to deportation.
  • If the court takes longer than 90 days to decide your asylum claim, or 180 days in a complex case, you can work legally with no application or fee, and you never wait more than 180 days from filing.
  • You have a qualified interpreter in the language you say you understand, papers that set a deadline are given to you in that language, and a signature on a document that was not translated for you cannot be used against you.
