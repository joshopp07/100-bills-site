The Aviation Safety Act

The Aviation Safety Act makes flying safer by counting close calls between aircraft and acting on them. Close calls are counted and published airport by airport every three months, and where an airport’s count climbs past a level drawn from the first year of counts, the FAA has to slow that airport’s arrivals or separate helicopters from airplanes until the count falls. Military aircraft near busy airports have to broadcast where they are. The busiest control facilities are staffed to a minimum set in law, by controllers guaranteed at least ten hours off between shifts. Pilots can seek care for depression, anxiety, or similar treatable conditions without that alone costing them the right to fly.

  • When an airport’s count of close calls rises above a level drawn from the first year of counts, the FAA has to cut that airport’s hourly arrivals or separate helicopter traffic from airplanes until the count falls back.
  • Every civilian aircraft required to broadcast its position also carries a cockpit display of the traffic around it.
  • Military aircraft flying near busy airports have to broadcast their position, with no exception.
  • The busiest air traffic control facilities have to be staffed to a minimum level set in law, with money to hire and train the controllers to reach it.
  • Today, a pilot who starts medicine for depression is grounded until the FAA decides the case, so some pilots avoid care. This bill lets pilots seek treatment for depression, anxiety, or a similar treatable condition without that alone costing them their medical certificate. Their doctor files a treatment plan showing how they stay safe to fly, the FAA clears it, and the pilot follows the plan.
