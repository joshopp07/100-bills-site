The Right to Quit Act

No federal rule on noncompete clauses is in effect today, so your freedom to change jobs depends on your state. The Right to Quit Act lets you leave a job without paying a price for it. You can take a better offer or start your own business, and your paperwork cannot make you pay, owe, or lose something for going, apart from the few narrow exceptions below. When Oregon banned noncompete clauses for low-wage hourly workers in 2008, hourly wages rose 2 to 3 percent on average with no effect on hours worked. An employer can still protect its trade secrets and confidential information, but never by limiting where or for whom you work.

  • You are covered whether you are an employee, a contractor, an intern, an apprentice, or a volunteer, whatever your title, pay, or immigration status, and the protections reach paperwork you have already signed.
  • A penalty for leaving is void whatever it is called, whether a repayment, a fee, or a forfeiture, and whether you quit or are let go.
  • Agreements between companies not to recruit or hire each other’s workers are void, including the no-poach clauses that appeared in 58 percent of major franchise companies’ disclosure documents in 2016.
  • You can be made to repay training only for a credential other employers recognize, that you chose, and that you would have paid for anyway, and what you owe shrinks to zero within two years.
  • The one noncompete left standing is a seller’s promise, made when selling a business in which the seller holds at least 25 percent, not to compete with the buyer for up to two years.
