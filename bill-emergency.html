The Emergency and War Powers Act

No President should be able to drift into an open-ended war, send troops into American streets, or use an emergency to do what Congress never approved. The Emergency and War Powers Act keeps a President’s power to repel an attack, rescue Americans in danger, and evacuate an embassy. But an operation abroad cannot escape the time limit on fighting without Congress by being called something other than war. The laws that let a President send troops into an American city come with defined conditions. And calling a group terrorists does not by itself authorize killing its members.

  • A President cannot use an emergency or a general law to impose tariffs, redirect public money, or regulate elections unless a law names that power.
  • To send in troops against a rebellion, a President needs an actual rebellion, one that is violent, organized, openly declared, and aimed at the federal government as a whole, which a court can check, and scattered violence at an otherwise peaceful protest does not count.
  • The President cannot take command of a state’s National Guard on the ground that federal law cannot be enforced while state or local police are still making arrests.
  • Sending combat troops into another country or firing on a foreign state’s forces counts as hostilities under the time limit on fighting without Congress’s approval, whatever officials call it, and a pause does not restart that clock.
  • Boats suspected of smuggling drugs are stopped under police rules, with deadly force allowed only to prevent death or serious injury, and no one shipwrecked, adrift, or surrendering can be attacked, whatever label the government uses.
