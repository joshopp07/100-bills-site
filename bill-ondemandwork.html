The On-Call Pay Act

In app work, waiting for the next customer is part of the job. A study of ride-hail work in one major city found that waiting for a request, or driving back from an outlying area, made up 29 percent of drivers’ working time. The On-Call Pay Act pays for that time when you choose to work for one company alone, and what you earn on jobs counts toward that guarantee. Any company can pay more. The choice to start a stretch is yours, and you can end it whenever no job is assigned. Turning down an offer ends the stretch, and you keep what you have already earned.

  • A company cannot shut you out of its app for more than a day without telling you in writing what you did, and two weeks ahead unless it involves violence, a credible threat of violence, sexual misconduct, or fraud.
  • You can be available through the app for at least 12 hours on every day it operates, and any other limit on your access takes two weeks’ written notice.
  • Once a stretch has begun, the company cannot end, pause, or shorten it, and you lose that stretch’s waiting pay only if you walked away in the middle of a job or took a job from another app.
  • The company cannot send you fewer jobs, pay you less per job, or limit your access because you chose to work for it alone, chose not to, claimed the pay you are owed, or took paid family or medical leave. Once the company knows you are on that leave, holding your absence against you counts as punishing you for the leave.
  • Unless you are already the company’s own employee, these protections cover you once it has used your work in at least six of the last 12 months and paid you at least $2,000, at any but the smallest companies. Cutting or ending your work to keep you from qualifying is unlawful, and a company whose records show it drops workers just before they qualify must prove it would have done the same anyway.
