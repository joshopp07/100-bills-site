The Accountable Government Act

The government has to show its work. Under the Accountable Government Act, a federal agency moving against you needs a named person to write down why, and you get a copy. If the writing never comes, the action does not count. An agency cannot hide behind a secret legal memo, and one that lets your case sit past its own stated time owes you answers. A federal employee ordered to act against a specific person can ask for that order in writing without being punished for asking. These rules bind whichever party holds power.

  • If an agency never produces a written reason for pulling your license, canceling your contract, or cutting off your benefit, the action does not count, and you keep what you had. In programs handling huge numbers of cases, an action taken without that writing does not fail right away: you first ask in writing for a proper decision, the action is on hold until the agency’s deadline to provide one, and if the deadline passes without one, the action does not count.
  • A computer program can help prepare a decision to pull your license or cancel your contract, but a named person has to make it, and simply agreeing with the program’s answer does not count as a decision.
  • If an agency reaches for a power it has not used in your kind of case in ten years, or uses one against you after leaving someone in your position alone within the past three years, it has to say why you are being treated differently.
  • If your case sits past the time the agency itself says a decision should take, the agency owes you a decision, or the facts about your case that are holding it up and a date.
  • The government cannot defend an action against you by pointing to a secret legal memo that you, Congress, and the public never saw.
