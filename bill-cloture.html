The Majority Rule Resolution

Today, ending a filibuster in the Senate takes 60 votes, so a bill most Senators support can die without a final vote. The Majority Rule Resolution lets the Senate act on what most of its members support. Every question the Senate’s rules reserve for more than a majority, from waiving a budget rule to changing the rules, is decided by a majority of the Senators voting, with the exception of votes to override vetoes, approve treaties, and convict in impeachment trials, which require supermajority votes in the Constitution and do not change. Each bill gets a fixed period of debate and then a vote, and the minority can still offer amendments of its choosing. In the House, the opening-day rules bring the new Congress’s first twenty-two bills to the floor.

  • A bill gets twelve hours on the Senate floor, divided equally between the two parties, and then a final vote.
  • Only the Senators who show up and vote are counted, so a Senator who skips a vote does not count against a bill.
  • On each bill, the minority party can call up two amendments of its choosing on the bill’s subject, with two hours of debate on each, and procedural moves cannot keep them off the floor.
  • Bills the House has already passed can come up in the Senate up to six at a time, sharing one twelve-hour period and two minority amendments, with a separate vote on each bill.
  • In the House, each of the first twenty-two bills of the new Congress gets one hour of debate, one motion to send it back to committee, and a final vote.
