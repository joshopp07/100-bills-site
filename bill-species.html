The Wildlife Habitat Act

The wildlife you hunt, fish, and watch cannot survive if its home is destroyed. The Wildlife Habitat Act writes that protection into the law itself: destroying habitat in a way that actually kills or injures a listed species counts as harming it, and no later rule can narrow that. A species whose listing decision runs past its legal deadline is protected until the decision comes. Every state, and federally recognized tribes, receives a yearly share of a permanent wildlife fund to help keep species off the endangered list. Landowners become partners in saving rare species instead of targets.

  • Wildlife crossings become a permanent national program, built first on the roads where published crash records show animals being hit. In Virginia, adding fencing at two underpasses cut deer crashes by 92 percent.
  • If you hold a conservation agreement or permit for a species and keep its terms, the government cannot ask you for more land, water, or money later because circumstances changed.
  • Conservation work you do on your land earns, once verified, credits you can sell or use toward mitigation requirements you face later.
  • A company that follows the published bird-safety practices for its industry, such as wind, power lines, or oil and gas, is not penalized when a bird is killed by accident, and one that knowingly breaks them can be.
  • A builder waiting on an overdue federal wildlife review can ask a court to order it finished within 60 days.
