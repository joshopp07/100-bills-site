The Water Shortage Act

The Water Shortage Act puts a price on using up water from rivers and aquifers that are drawn down faster than they refill, and pays those who volunteer to use less. Where a river or aquifer is short, farms, cities, and businesses pay a charge on each acre-foot they use up, and the government buys verified cutbacks. The Colorado River is the leading case, but the bill reaches every critically short area. When rates are first set, the charge is set to balance the cost of the purchases. Cities and farms face smaller and fairer cuts when supplies fall.

  • Farms, cities, and businesses pay alike for each acre-foot they use up, and the charge rises as a shortage deepens, with no charge where water levels and use are sound.
  • The government buys verified cutbacks from those who volunteer, for a season, for several years, or for good, taking the offers that cost least per acre-foot first.
  • Water a farmer verifiably saves cannot be forfeited under state use-it-or-lose-it law.
  • A Tribe pays nothing on its own use within its water right and can sell verified cutbacks on the same terms as any other seller, and formal consultation with Tribes comes before these terms are written into a bill.
  • A county decides before more than a quarter of its irrigated land is retired for good, and part of the money helps the county the water leaves.
