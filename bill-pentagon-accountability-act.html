The Pentagon Accountability Act

The Pentagon Accountability Act lets any American see where the military’s money goes, and it stops the government from paying prices no one ever tested. The money Congress gives the armed forces is traced, in one public record, from the vote that provides it to the final payment, and the Defense Secretary personally signs for the books. Where only one company can supply what the military needs, the Pentagon sees that company’s costs before agreeing to its price, and it is able to buy spare parts from a second source. Purchases of anything actually sold on the open market stay fast.

  • Anyone can follow the money Congress provides the military from the vote that provides it to the final payment, in one public record updated on a fixed schedule.
  • The Defense Secretary and each military financial officer personally sign for the books, a missing signature is published on the day it is due, and an office that does not sign loses its power to move money between accounts until it signs.
  • Even a secret program appears in the public record with its dollar amount and account, while its description stays classified.
  • A sole supplier, or a seller whose bid was the only one, owes the Pentagon its certified costs on a purchase above $2 million, counted across a year so a large order cannot be split into small ones to avoid it.
  • Every company bidding on a weapon has to name a price for the designs the military needs to have spare parts made by someone else, so the military is not locked into one supplier.
