The Long-Term Care Act

Today, Medicaid must cover a nursing home, but most care at home is optional for states. Almost 650,000 people are on state waiting or interest lists for home care, and those who got services waited an average of 34 months. This bill lets older Americans and people with disabilities who qualify for a nursing home choose care at home instead. Medicaid adds it as a benefit that a state taking the bill’s added federal money must provide, and the same rule reaches Medicare and private health plans. No one is forced to leave a nursing home. If the One Medicare Act passes, these protections move with Medicaid into Medicare.

  • If Medicare or a private plan would cover you in a nursing home, it can no longer refuse care at home by calling it custodial, by requiring you to be homebound, or by requiring a hospital stay first.
  • A state taking the bill’s added federal money cannot cap how many people receive care at home, limit it to certain counties or diagnoses, or hold it to a stricter standard than a nursing home.
  • In a state taking the bill’s added federal money, you can choose a family member, including a spouse, as your paid caregiver, with that pay free of income tax, and a person cared for by an unpaid family member is entitled to at least 30 days of respite care a year.
  • With a referral, anyone covered by Medicare, Medicaid, or a private plan who has trouble with daily activities, whether or not they qualify for a nursing home, can get help from a nurse and an occupational therapist and up to $20,000 per referral for home changes and devices.
  • If the One Medicare Act also passes, the federal program owes this care in every state, a person whose household earns no more than the median wage, with savings under about $160,000 not counting most homes, pays no premium and no cost sharing for assessed long-term care, and nothing is recovered from your estate.
