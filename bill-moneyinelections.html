The Money in Elections Amendment

In 1976 and again in 2010, the Supreme Court struck down limits on what candidates and outside spenders may spend on elections, so no ordinary law can set them. The Money in Elections Amendment lets Congress and the states set reasonable, even-handed limits on election spending and require disclosure of whose money is behind the ads voters see. They can also fund voluntary public financing, and states can limit spending on ballot questions. The aim is to clean up elections and make big donors’ influence in politics smaller. Each new limit or disclosure rule still has to become law. No government can use the amendment to tell anyone what to say about a candidate or a cause.

  • Congress and the states can set reasonable, even-handed limits on money spent to influence an election, including a candidate’s own fortune and money spent by groups outside any campaign.
  • An ad promoting or attacking a candidate can be covered by those rules whether or not it says "vote for" or "vote against."
  • Congress and the states can run voluntary public financing that gives a participating candidate more public money when an opponent or an outside group outspends them, and no candidate who declines to join can be kept off the ballot.
  • Your state can limit the money spent for or against a question on your ballot and require disclosure of whose money paid for the ads.
  • If you give to a cause and can show a court a reasonable probability of threats or reprisals, a disclosure law passed under the amendment cannot force you to be named.
