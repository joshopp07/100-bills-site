The Congressional Terms Amendment

The Constitution sets no limit on how long anyone may serve in Congress, and in 1995 the Supreme Court held that a state cannot impose one. The Congressional Terms Amendment writes one in: eighteen years in total, House and Senate combined. The aim is to keep career politicians from entrenching themselves and to keep representative government dynamic. No one can be elected or appointed to a term that would carry them past the limit, so reaching it never cuts a term short. A candidate over the limit cannot appear on your ballot. Years served in terms that began before ratification do not count, and Congress cannot loosen the limit by passing a law.

  • No one can be elected, or appointed to fill a Senate vacancy, for a term that would carry their total time in Congress past eighteen years.
  • Eighteen years allows up to three full Senate terms, nine full House terms, or a mix totaling no more than eighteen years.
  • A candidate barred by the limit cannot appear on the ballot in a primary, runoff, special, or general election, and if one wins anyway, the seat is treated as empty and filled the usual way.
  • A member a court finds to be over the limit loses the seat, and votes cast from that seat afterward do not count.
